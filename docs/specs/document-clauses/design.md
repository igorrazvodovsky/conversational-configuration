# Document clauses — design

Rules how the customer's document is held: one list of clauses with identity, the three kinds distinguished by the facts a clause carries, where a reconciliation mark lands, and which of the prompt's two sorting rules covers the `safety` group. Read it before adding a field to the `rfq` block, before changing what `ingest_rfq` is given, and before moving a group between document layers.

Status: implemented. It repairs findings 6, 7, 9 and 10 of the [ontology](../ontology-of-phenomena/ontology.md). Verification and known gaps are at the end.

## Decision 1: one list, three kinds, told apart by facts rather than by which list they are in

The record holds `rfq.clauses`, one entry per clause of the document. Which kind a clause is follows from the facts it carries, exactly as the [ontology](../ontology-of-phenomena/ontology.md) states them:

| A clause | `carries` | `requires` | In the record |
|---|---|---|---|
| that states a value of a term the model has | yes | yes | `variable` and `value`, plus a `reconciliation` mark |
| that leaves the decision to us | yes | no | `variable`, no `value`, no mark |
| that no product variable carries | no | no | neither, plus a `note` saying why |

The alternative was to keep two lists and add a third for the clause left to us. It was rejected because the demote path is what finding 7 is about: an entry that could not be mapped moved between lists, so the same clause was two kinds of thing at two moments of one call, and a third list would have added a second demotion rather than removing the first. With one list, mapping is *which facts a clause gains*, and failing to map is gaining fewer. Nothing moves.

The kinds are read through two predicates rather than by testing keys at each call site — `requirement(clause)` with `requirements(config)`, `clauses_left_to_us(config)` and `unmapped_clauses(config)` on the Python side, `requirements(config)` and `clausesLeftToUs(config)` on the frontend — so a fourth kind, if one ever arrives, arrives in one place.

## Decision 2: identity is minted once, persisted, and never read off the document

A clause's identity is a uuid, minted when the clause comes into existence, and it is not the clause number. The number is a value the document supplies: documents repeat numbers across sections, omit them in prose paragraphs, and two clauses in the fixtures already share a variable. An identity the document controls would make two clauses one whenever the customer's numbering is loose, which is the failure finding 6 records in a different form. Deriving one from the clause's content — its citation and its quote together — fails the same way and for a reason the paper states directly: an identity can be *matched* but not compared or decomposed, so an identity computed from values is not one.

*Minted once* is the other half. The paper asks an individual for a persistent identity, and a uuid minted afresh on each read is not persistent: two reads of one document would disagree about which clauses they hold. What that costs is concrete rather than tidy. The store computes a draft's log by diffing the configuration a tool hands back against the one it reads from disk, so two disagreeing reads make the next ordinary edit log a retraction and re-assertion of every clause — a phantom rewrite of the document, which the reversal then describes to the customer as the document having just been seeded. Every door a configuration arrives through therefore mints at most once; decision 3 says how each of them does it.

That puts a Clause with Workspace, Draft and Entry: created at runtime, identified by a uuid, durable in the record. Reconciling one never deletes it — reconciled is not gone, which the [rfq-reconciliation spec](../rfq-reconciliation/requirements.md) already fixes — but it is not an individual that outlives everything. `ingest_rfq` is a content action, so undoing it retracts every clause fact and the clauses cease with it; redoing it brings them back under the identities the log holds.

The ontology's individual is renamed from `Requirement` to `Clause`, and the value formerly called `Clause` — the number the document gives, `"5.2"` — becomes `Citation`, carried by the record's `clause` key. `Requirement` misdescribed two kinds of three, and an individual and a value under one name is the collision that makes the rename cheap to justify. Nothing persisted is renamed by it: `Requirement` was a TypedDict name and a word in prose, and neither rides in a checkpoint.

## Decision 3: `requirements` and `unmapped` are retired as names, and the record is lifted at read time

The durable key is `rfq.clauses`. `requirements` would have named a list two of whose three kinds require nothing, which is the argument the [ontology's names-already-spent rule](../ontology-of-phenomena/requirements.md) asks to be made here rather than assumed. What that rule protects is resumption — a key or a tool name a written record already spends — and the lift below is what pays for the change instead.

`_adapt` in `agent/src/workspace_store.py` is where it happens, beside the two lifts already there. A record written before this change holds `rfq.requirements` and `rfq.unmapped`; both are read, each entry keeps every value it has, an identity is minted for it, and the record is written back in the new shape *by the read that lifted it*. Nothing is synthesized: an old requirement already carries variable, value, clause, quote and mark, and an old unmapped entry already carries clause, quote and note. Only the identity is new, and an identity is the one thing that can be minted honestly, since it says nothing about the document.

Writing back on the read rather than leaving it to the next write is what makes the minting happen once, which decision 2 requires. `_adapt` reports whether it converted anything and `_read` persists the record when it did, so every later read returns the identities the first one minted. A read that changes nothing stays a read. The other conversions the adapter performs — dropping frames, letting snapshots and pre-identity log entries lapse — are persisted by the same write, which costs nothing and settles them too.

That is a departure from the precedent `_adapt` itself records — frames and history snapshots were let lapse rather than converted — and the reason the precedent does not apply to a *configuration* is that those conversions would have had to invent provenance, where this one invents nothing.

It does apply to the draft's *log*, which is the other half and was nearly missed. An entry written before this change states a clause's facts in a vocabulary with no clause identity in it — `requires` carrying the citation and the quote inside it, `reconciled` addressed by the variable — and `rebuild` now raises on a fact of a clause nothing cites. Converting one would mean naming an identity that did not exist when the entry was written, which is the frames argument exactly. So an entry carrying those facts is dropped and every other entry in the same log survives: an ordinary revision moves `chose` and `attributed`, which this change did not touch, and it stays reversible beside a lifted document. The entries are told apart by shape rather than by a stored version — `requires` and `note` by the arity identity changed, `reconciled` by whether its subject is an identity at all — because a record carries no schema version to ask.

A record is not the only door a configuration comes through. The tools read `runtime.state`, which is a thread checkpoint the store's adapter has never seen, so a conversation resumed on a thread written before this change would hand them two lists — and every reader of the register indexes `clauses`. `_get_config` brings that up to shape on the way in, and the rule it follows is decision 2's: it does not mint. The workspace record has already minted these clauses once and persisted them, so when a workspace is attached the block is taken from the record, which is the durable locus of state anyway (constitution #7). Minting a second set here would hand the store a document it does not recognise, and the next edit would log the phantom rewrite decision 2 describes. A conversation with no workspace behind it has nothing to disagree with, so there the block is lifted in the ordinary way — minting is both safe and the only way to read it at all.

The claim the predicates rest on is therefore that both doors lift, and it is checked at both, as is the identity each door produces.

`ingest_rfq` takes one `clauses` argument in place of `requirements` and `unmapped`. Tool *names* ride in thread checkpoints and are spent; a tool call's arguments are a historical record that nothing re-executes, so the parameter names are not. The prompt teaches the new shape in the same session, which is the coupling constitution #15 makes explicit.

## Decision 4: a mark lands on the clauses a move answers, not on every clause of the variable

`reconcile_requirement` still takes a variable and still makes one answer to one disagreement — settlement 4 of the ontology is unchanged, and no gesture is added. What changed is which clauses the mark lands on. A move now marks each clause on that variable by what the move does to it:

- a clause whose requested value is where the agreement lands has nothing to answer, and keeps the mark it had — `pending` for a clause nobody has answered, which is what unanswered means, and whatever it was left at for one that has been. A mark the agreement has since overtaken is not rewritten, because the register tests met-ness before the mark and so already reads such a clause as met;
- a clause whose value differs takes the move's mark — `waived` on accept, `revised` on revise, `pending` on open.

Before, every clause on the variable took the same mark, so on a document asking two different speeds, accepting the offered value marked the clause the agreement *meets* as waived. The register hid the worst of it by testing met-ness before the mark, so the row read correctly while the record behind it did not. The rule above is the code's own `revise` special case — the one that already marked `pending` when the value moved to is what every clause asked for — generalised from all-or-nothing to per clause, which is what identity buys.

What the tool reports follows the same split, and it needs a sentence of its own for the case where the move answers no clause at all. When every clause on the term asks for the value being applied, the customer has brought the agreement into line with their own document: nothing was waived and nothing revised, and the term is met. This is the ordinary case rather than an exotic one — a single-clause requirement the customer resolves by picking the value their tender asked for reaches it, and the sheet dispatches a *revise* for that click because the document asks something of the row. Reporting it as a revision named a move that had not happened, and, having no clause to cite, named it with an empty citation. It now reads *Clause 4.1 is now met: Service level is …. Nothing was waived or revised.* The `accept` move cannot reach this case, because `reconcile` refuses a waiver when the agreement already meets every clause — there is nothing to waive.

## Decision 5: `safety` is an outcome term, in the prompt and on the sheet

The prompt sorted a document's clauses two ways — outcome terms it works from, hardware figures it derives and asks about — and `rescue_operation` and `firefighters_operation` were in neither. Both now fall under the outcome rule, and `LAYER_OF_GROUP` gains `safety: "terms"`, so the two artifacts sort the same clause the same way.

The alternative was to extend the hardware rule, which is what the group's position in the collapsed schedules implied. It was rejected on what the agent would then say: the hardware rule has the agent answer with *what is that figure there to achieve*, and a firefighters' lift is not a figure standing in for an outcome. It is an obligation the building code imposes and the customer has no discretion over, so the question has no answer they can give, and asking it reads as pushing back on a regulation. The [rfq-reconciliation spec's](../rfq-reconciliation/requirements.md) stage boundary widens by one group as a result: a stage-1 document may speak `agreement`, `context`, `performance` and `safety`, and a document that enters `platform`, `dimensions`, `doors` or `cabin` is still stage two.

`accessibility` was already a compliance term in `context`, rendered in the recitals, and one fixture already states EN 81-70 as a requirement. So the sheet was already carrying a regulatory obligation in a customer-legible layer, and `safety` sitting in the schedules was the inconsistency rather than the precedent.

## Decision 6: a term the document speaks to without asking anything edits

The canvas routes a click on a term to a reconciliation or to a hidden canvas edit *per variable*, and it decides from the register. A term carrying only a clause left to us has no register row, so it edits — which is the only thing it can do: `reconcile_requirement` answers a deviation, and a clause that asks for nothing raises there rather than being answered.

That makes the rule three cases where it was two, and the rule is stated in three places — `CLAUDE.md`, the [agreement-document design](../agreement-document/design.md) and the [rfq-reconciliation design](../rfq-reconciliation/design.md) — each of which said *a term the RFQ speaks to reconciles, every other one edits*. All three now say what the document has to do for a term to reconcile: ask something of it. Choosing a value on a term the document left to us is the customer answering a question, not conceding one, and the sheet's silence is right for it.

## Decision 7: what a clause left to us is called, and what shows it

*Left to us*, in the tool's report to the agent, in the prompt paragraph that tells it what to record, and on the sheet: "Clause 5.2 leaves this to us", parallel to the register's "Clause 3.1 asked". *Us* is the supplier, which is the voice the agreement document and the agent already use.

The word retired to make room is *gap*. It named two phenomena — the variables ingestion reports as still open, which the ontology calls `undecided`, and this clause, which had no name because it had no record. Every use in the prompt, the tools and this feature's neighbours now names one or the other.

On the sheet, `LeftToUsMark` is a peer of `DeviationMark` in `document-parts.tsx`, rendered in the same margin, in all three layers. It is a mark rather than a register row because a clause that asks nothing has nothing to compare: no requested value, no offered value, no rule separating them, and no move that answers it on the document. What answers it is the conversation the agent opens, which is why the same clause is reported to the agent at ingestion.

The mark is a component rather than a shared class string, unlike the layer headings the [chat-surface design](../chat-surface/design.md) rules on. It sits in the same slot `DeviationMark` already occupies, mints no id, and renders identically on the server and the first client pass, so it is the safe case that decision's last paragraph names rather than the one it forbids.

## Verification

Constitution #9. `uv run pytest` in `agent/` and `npm test` with `npm run typecheck` at the root, all green: 283 agent tests and 292 frontend tests at the baseline taken before the change, 303 and 293 after it, with typecheck clean throughout.

What the checks reach: the three kinds surviving ingestion, a clause the model cannot carry keeping its citation and its quote, a clause left to us seeding nothing and entering no register row, every clause carrying an identity of its own, per-clause marking on a document that asks two values of one term, the read-time lift of a record written as two lists and its idempotence, that lift persisting so two reads return one set of identities and a record needing no conversion is not rewritten, an ordinary edit on a lifted record logging only itself, a thread checkpoint written before the change taking the record's identities rather than minting a second set, an entry written in the old fact vocabulary lapsing while an ordinary revision beside it stays reversible, the trace vocabulary's round trip and per-clause mark, a reversal naming the term a marked clause bears on and a move that answers no clause reporting the term as met rather than as revised, the ingestion report naming the clauses left to us, the register omitting a clause that asks nothing, `safety` landing in the operative terms, and `tests/couplings.test.ts` holding the ontology's names against the code's — which is what caught the ontology mid-change and made reconciling it a failing run rather than a reader's job.

Two things the checks do not reach. The extraction itself: whether the agent puts a clause left to us in `clauses` with a variable and no value is a matter of the prompt, and only the paid scenario runs see a prompt edit. And the rendering: `safety` moving into the operative terms changes the sheet's shape, and the new margin mark is markup no check looks at, so both were verified by running the app against a workspace seeded with all three kinds of clause. Entrapment rescue and firefighters' operation render as numbered operative terms, the firefighters' clause carrying its document provenance; "Clause 5.2 leaves this to us" renders in the margin of the term it bears on, beside an unaffected deviation mark; and the console reports no hydration mismatch on load or on reload, which is the check the [chat-surface design](../chat-surface/design.md) requires of anything joining this page's tree. The two 500s in the console are CopilotKit's thread endpoints answering for a conversation that has not been registered yet, reproduced on an empty workspace with no document at all.

*Seven mutations were run*, in the form the [action-log design](../action-log/design.md) established, and each was caught by the check for it. The log half of the lift was disabled at its call site; the checkpoint lift was removed from `_get_config`; the write-back that persists the lift was removed from `_read`; `_get_config` was put back to minting instead of taking the record's clauses; `describe_delta` was put back to reading a `reconciled` fact's second argument as a variable; the branch that reports a term brought into line was disabled; and the doubled possessive was restored to `ingest_rfq`'s move phrase.

Two of those guard against a check that passes for the wrong reason. A test may assert that a description omits a term the model has dropped, and pass because the lookup returns nothing for *every* input — so the check that a mark which moved *is* described sits beside it, and the same test asserts the empty result without the map. Reading a record twice with a write in between hides a per-read minting entirely, so the identity check reads twice with no write.

*The paid runs were made, 2026-08-22.* `pytest -m scenario` and `compare_refs.py HEAD` over all five scenarios. What they show about this change:

- Ingestion still lands one call carrying every clause — `the_document_is_ingested_once`, `the_agreement_is_seeded_from_the_document` and `every_requirement_carries_its_clause` pass on both trees, so the one-list signature costs the extraction nothing.
- `the_accepted_requirement_is_waived_not_dropped` passes here and fails on the earlier tree, which keeps no log for it to read. Structural rather than behavioural, and expected.
- The `safety` rule is still exercised by nothing, since neither fixture states a safety clause. Unchanged by the runs and still the gap recorded below.
- One tender assertion, `revising_is_one_reconcile_call`, failed on one run of this tree and passed on the other, with the earlier tree passing both times. It is a prose turn and it flaps; the [one-gesture-one-action design](../one-gesture-one-action/design.md) records the evidence that the flapping assertions are nondeterministic rather than regressions.

Running the comparison at all required repairing the harness first: its assertions indexed `rfq["clauses"]`, so the arm running the earlier agent died on a `KeyError` and the comparison returned nothing. The check that exists to see a prompt edit could not run across the change that rewrote the prompt. Reading the clauses now goes through one reader that accepts either shape ([conversation-checks](../conversation-checks/design.md)).

## Known gaps

- *A clause left to us is visible only where a variable carries it.* If the extraction cannot name the variable, the clause is an unmapped one and the sheet shows it nowhere, which is the position unmapped clauses were already in. Making the unmapped list visible on the document is a separate change; nothing here regresses it.
- *No gesture answers a clause left to us.* The agent raises it in conversation and the customer answers by choosing a value, which reaches `revise_choices` like any other choice. The clause keeps no mark saying it was answered, so the record cannot yet distinguish a clause the customer decided from one nobody has raised. That is the substrate the pending [open-points spec](../open-points/requirements.md) needs, and it is the same shape as ontology finding 5.
- *The document's own fixtures state no safety clause.* Both RFQs in `agent/fixtures/rfq/` predate decision 5, so the prompt's new rule about safety obligations is exercised by nothing, paid runs included. Adding a clause to a fixture changes what every scenario run over it asserts, which is why it is named here rather than done in passing.
- *The stage boundary is stated in two places.* The [rfq-reconciliation spec](../rfq-reconciliation/requirements.md) names the stage-1 groups in prose and `LAYER_OF_GROUP` names them in code, and decision 5 moved both. Nothing holds them together, so the next group that moves can move in one place only.
